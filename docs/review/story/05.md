# 05 · Chapter 5: The Cackling Chasm (story review)

*Judged against the game-narrative-writing skill (Seger, Mills, Berger). Scene numbers and quotes are from `docs/review/script/05.md`; the design is `story_bible.md`, "Chapter 5". Line, staging and bug findings are in `docs/review/notes/05.md` and are cited by E-number, not repeated.*

## 1. The chapter in one line

**As the player would put it:** *Will we let Granny kill a yeti cub to cure Reptin and end the winter?* **No. We find a third way. Reptin keeps the wolf as a charge, the acorn takes the stolen time instead of a life, the twins hatch, and the world tips over.**

**Does the game say the question?** Yes, and more sharply than the bible's "Can we stop her?", which nobody ever asks. Chapter 4 puts the bargain ("Do a little task for Granny, dearies, and I'll end the winter. And I'll take the wolf out of him."), and the climax asks it outright: "Well, my dear wolves... will you honour our deal? Or do you seek to renegotiate?" (5.20). It's a choice between two wrongs with an innocent as the price (Mills, p. 129).

**Does it answer the last chapter's question?** Yes. Chapter 4 answers "Who has been calling us?" and leaves "Come and find me in the chasm, dearies. Bring your wolf." and Durmoot's "make sure she never comes back up out of it." Chapter 5 takes both. In its last minute it also raises Chapter 6's question ("A sapling. A forest. A city. Four hundred years, all at once.").

**What it drops.** The bible's throughline is "who called the Heralds north, and *why*". Chapter 4 gave the why: the relics were lures, and Thredra is Auril's "seamstress" fitting a new coat. At the climax she only wants "the new Winter... Not so fickle as Auril." The vessel plan gets half a line ("My little seamstress's samples, all in a row"), and the one bridge, "She promised Auril a gift. A big one." / "I don't think it's the gift Auril wanted.", belongs to Rhosk, an optional trainer. The act climax answers "who" and forgets "why" (problem 3).

**Gameplay vs text.** Gameplay carries the obstacles and the climax, and the fights carry story: the troll heals unless burned (the ghost's "only flame" tip), the locket counters Thredra's evasion and her three-of-her illusion, and the yeti you calmed fights beside you. Text carries the opening, both turning points, the midpoint and the resolution.

**Game-wide milestones here.**

- **Act I climax** (Berger's obligatory scene, p. 85–86): 5.20–5.24. It lands as a dilemma and a fight, and falls short as the hero's act.
- **The Reversal** (5.24–5.27, finishing in 6.01) is **the game's first turning point** in Seger's sense: a new direction, a new arena, a new question, no way back (p. 45–47). That lands. It is **not yet Berger's point of no return**, which is the hero's first heroic act *committing* them to a mission (p. 81–82). The heroic act here (refusing the bargain) commits the party to nothing; the Reversal happens *to* them as a side effect, and no Act II mission exists until 6.25. See `06.md` §2.
- **Proportions, for the final pass.** Act I is Chapters 1–5 of 13 (levels 5–33 of about 70), about 38% of the game. Seger's Act One is about a quarter (p. 35–36), and Berger wants a short Act 1 in games (p. 90).

## 7. Game fit

- **Story is a reward in the dungeon, and a wall at the ends.** Each obstacle pays a beat: the fork with Shade's grief, the bridge with the voices, the gate with the vision (Berger, p. 34–35). The walls are the sanctum (38 boxes before a choice), the parley (33) and the ending (43) (E7; Berger, p. 111).
- **Scene and sequel** fit Mills (p. 108–109) in the dungeon: the pit is the disaster, "Hags know everything you're scared of. That is their whole job." is the sequel. At the act's end, the sequel to "time comes loose" is 23 loot boxes, which is the wrong size (Mills, p. 152).
- **The conflict matches the gameplay**, and that's the chapter's best feature. The ghost's gifts become battle mechanics (the locket, the flame), the calmed yeti fights beside you, and the fork and the gate are puzzles made out of the theme (Despain via the skill).
- **Meaningful choices.** The yeti, the bridge and the parley pass Berger's test (p. 94). "...Do it." is the best choice in Act I, and the loop remembers it ("You were so sensible, last time round."). Two fail: "Watch her closely" (nothing reads it) and "Break it" (refused by a voice: a false choice, Berger p. 36). Give "Break it" a cost (the Heart cracks, and the Ch8 Forge has less to burn) or cut it.
- **The optional midpoint** breaks the rule that critical beats sit on the mandatory spine and required information never hides in optional-looking content (DeMarle and Bateman, IGDA, via the skill). The hollow wall is optional-looking.

## 8. Verdict

**What's working.**

1. **The bargain is a real dilemma, and the dark path makes it bite.** Set up a chapter early, asked at the climax, and the wrong answer plays out in full before the wheel turns back (Mills, p. 129; Berger, p. 94).
2. **Reptin's arc is shown, not told**: five beats, nearly all actions, ending "It knows me now."
3. **Theme as mechanic**: the illusion fork, the life-and-death bowls, the ghost's gifts in battle.
4. **The act break is built to be remembered**: "...Hi.", "That is sap.", and the fall through four hundred years to your own face in a lantern.

**Top 3 story problems.**

**1. The midpoint, the climax's rule and Reptin's choice live in an optional room.** *Why it hurts:* skip the sanctum and the climax's "A life that hasn't started yet." comes from nowhere, and Reptin goes from tempted to settled with no decision. Every payoff must be set up (Seger, p. 148–150); critical beats go on the mandatory spine (Berger, p. 44). *Fix:* make the sanctum the way to the Heart (E3), then split the ghost into two beats with an action between (E7): beat one ends on "Because somebody has to hold it."; you take the dagger and locket from the bier; beat two gives the rule and the tips. Keep "Time is a wheel, dear." as her exit.

**2. At the climax the player watches.** *Why it hurts:* after a chapter of good choices, the staff breaks itself, the last mirror breaks in narration, and Alberica solves the act. Everything the journey taught (Shimmer's third way, the ghost's rule, Mishann's acorn) is in the player's bag. The hero must win with what the journey taught (Mills, p. 132); catharsis belongs to the protagonist (Berger, p. 111); the decision beat reveals the most (Seger, p. 251). *Fix:* the sketch below. Alberica's hands stay on the root, because 6.16 needs her to have "anchored" the Reversal, but the player gives the order.

**3. Thredra's motive splits, and the throughline's "why" vanishes.** *Why it hurts:* Ch4's seamstress fitting a coat for Auril and Ch5's "Not her winter. Mine." never meet on the golden path, so "why were we called?" ends as "for herself, apparently", and Alberica's rune goes quiet for the act's climax. An antagonist's goal must be clear and directly opposed (Seger, p. 227); a big reveal needs consequences (Berger, p. 112–113). *Fix:* one exchange after "Not her winter. Mine.": "ALBERICA: You said you were her seamstress." / "THREDRA: And I've fitted her a lovely coat, dearie. *(a look at Alberica's chest)* I'll hand it over. Once I'm wearing the winter myself." That keeps the rune live into Act II. Then: "THREDRA: Thank you for the door, dearies. Only a wolf could open it." (E14). Move Rhosk's "I don't think it's the gift Auril wanted." to an unskippable trainer at the cave mouth.

**Rewrite sketch: the last mirror and the third way (5.23–5.24).** This is the act climax and the weakest important scene, because the player only watches it. New inventions are marked *(suggestion)*.

1. **The Reflection falls.** *On screen:* you, the party as stand-ins round you (E1), and Mirror 7 at (12,12) in your path, with Thredra visible inside it. *Player:* walks up to it.
2. **The player breaks the last mirror.** CHOICE "Break it." / "Break it with the Order's dagger." *(suggestion: the dagger's only use: "Fey things don't like it.")* E-06 on the glass; "laughing in one, weeping in another, silent in a third." *Changes:* the player ends the hag, not narration.
