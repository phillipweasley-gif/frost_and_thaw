# Part 10 story review · Chapter 10: Trials of the Roots

Scope: `docs/review/script/10.md` (43 played scenes, 10.01–10.43, plus 12 reachable-but-unplayed blocks, 10.X1–10.X12). The Tribunal itself (charges, arrest) is in Part 9 (9.10–9.11), so it's cited from there. "Notes §En" refers to `docs/review/notes/10.md`. Book cites use the page numbers in the skill's reference files.

The test for this chapter is the one the owner set: **does each Trial pay off what that Herald actually did on screen earlier?** The short answer: two of five do (Meat Meat, Wyn), one half-does (Reptin), and two try something the player never saw (Alberica) or nothing at all (Alerion). Meanwhile the three biggest things the party really did in Act II go untried: Glimmer killed the engine, Avarice faked her death, and Selrae let the Halo in for thirty years.

---

## 1. The chapter in one line

**As the player would put it from the script alone:** *"Will the city convict us for killing Summer, and who fills the empty chairs?"* **Answer:** *"No. We're acquitted of everything, Meat Meat goes free, and Rheven and Eirwen take the chairs."*

**Does the game ever say the question?** It states the procedure, not the question. The crier gives the agenda ("to witness the trial of the slayers of Summer! And to fill two empty chairs!", 10.02), and Kelyn gives the rules in 9.11 ("You have seven days to bring witnesses and evidence"). The bible's question, **"Were we right?"**, is never asked in those words or any others on the played path. The nearest the played path gets is Kelyn in 9.10 ("Half of them think you saved them. Half think you killed it."). The line that actually asks it is in content the run never reached, the debate-stage heckler (10.X4): "Summer's dead, and they did it with no trial! Who's next? Who do they decide doesn't deserve a season?" That's the chapter's real question, and it's locked in an optional stage.

**Does it answer the question the last chapter raised?** Yes, at the plot level. Chapter 9 ends on the arrest ("There will be a trial. The Trials of the Roots. ...I'm sorry.", 9.10), so "will they be convicted?" is a real hook and Chapter 10 answers it. At the theme level ("were we right to kill without the Council?"), the answer is given but never earned. The verdict rules that "Her death was necessary" (10.42) after a one-box witness list. Nobody argues the other side once the week starts, the acquittal is never in doubt (26 witnesses; the Summer charge scored 24 against the 8 needed), and the death it excuses didn't happen: the player may have just seen Avarice's decoy body (8.23, "Avarice's arms were covered in burned sigils. These have none.") and her face in Autumn's mirror (10.16, "a woman with white hair turns and looks out at you"). So the chapter answers "were we right?" with "yes, obviously" about a killing it has already told the player was faked.

**Trial-level spines.** Two trials have a full small shape. **Roots of Love** (10.11–10.12): catalyst "...but what have you become?", development (three memory and honesty choices), a test (the Heartroot), a climax vision, and a resolution ("You're protecting it. Not replacing it.") with a reward (the treant). **Assassin's Reckoning** (10.15–10.16) has a catalyst (the confession), a twist (Phildendrin), a mission (the mirror), and a climax (the wraith). **Absent Watcher** (10.17–10.22) has a shape, but its climax happens to Wyn with Wyn silent ("Reprimanded. The post is yours" → no reply). **Brother from Beyond** (10.23) skips straight to its climax: in the run, "Decide what to do" was offered before any investigation. **Spores of Contention** (10.24–10.25) has a structure, but its climax belongs to a different story (the memory bubble is the Summer case, not the spores).

**Game-wide milestones (Berger, pp. 81–87, 134–136) in this part.**
- **End of Act II.** The bible calls the Trials "the climax of Act II". Berger's Act II pattern of **exposure of weakness + loss of trust** (p. 83) fits the arrest exactly: the Heralds' weakness (killing without the Council, Meat Meat's choice on the bridge) costs them the city's trust. It **doesn't land**, because the week never makes the loss of trust felt. The city's hostility is a few gossips and an invisible "mud" count, and trust comes back by default.
- **The ticking clock.** The seven days are this chapter's clock (Mills's ticking clock, p. 192). It **doesn't tick**: the whole run played on "Day 2" and Kelyn let it call the verdict there ("Day 2 of seven", 10.41; notes §E2).
- **Seed of Act III's ticking time bomb** (Berger p. 85). The pilgrim dream (10.07: "Take a little cold with you... It keeps things the way they are" / "Somebody says 'Sephek' out loud in the dark") and Selrae's warning (10.40) plant Chapter 11's Frost Saint. The Selrae line **lands**. The dream lands as menace but leaves a name the player can't place (notes, first-time player 9).
- **Obligatory scene / climax** for Act II is the Verdict, and it's the weakest scene in the part (section 8).

**Carried by gameplay vs text.** Gameplay carries the city chores (wall jobs, the wolf double battle), the Underdark crawl, Autumn's mirror wraith, the Wall trip, and Gym 8. Text carries every judgment: the trials' outcomes, the verdict and the vote. That split is the chapter's core game-fit problem (section 7). The things the chapter is *about* (persuading a city, being judged) are all text, while the fights are mostly wild battles that have nothing to do with the case.

### The player character

**Decisions the player makes in this part.** *With visible consequences:* refuse or take the pilgrim's cold blessing (10.07); Reptin's three answers to Anduin (10.11: reconciled / doubtful / leaving); the Heartroot path, Hyphora's answer and how to pluck the leaf (10.12: the vision, the treant); Meat Meat stays, slips out or breaks out (10.15: mud, battles, outlaw risk); Wyn's account and whom to call (10.18: believed / barred); Talion released, ritual or held (10.23: three endings, two unplayed); how to answer the spores councillors (10.24: colony recognised or exiled); bargain with or burn the Undercity colony (10.30: Rootwardens angered); endorse, stand or stay neutral (10.41); call the verdict early (10.41: unsignposted; open trials are lost). *Hidden or cosmetic:* trial order (no clock, notes §E2); the wall jobs and blankets (support points no one mentions); Meat Meat's apology wording; asking Phildendrin to stand (a petition the run never filled); "Who speaks for the Heralds" (10.42: a hidden score); the two endorsements (10.43: they decide the Act III crossing, which isn't said, and the run's second pick was overruled in silence).

**Battles they could lose:** the wolves (retry), the Underdark ambush (retry), Autumn's mirror wraith, the Time Wight ("the story goes on win or lose"), Gym 8, and the optional wardens. None of them changes the trial.

**Done to them:** the arrest and the charges (Part 9); four omens (they happen to Alberica, not the player); the pilgrim dream; the Shard of Stillness appearing in your pocket; the verdict and the vote outcomes.

**Active or passenger?** Busy, but a passenger where it counts. The player does a great deal in this chapter and decides the most important moments of four other characters' trials: Meat Meat's stay-or-run, Reptin's answer to the tree, Wyn's defence, Alerion's goodbye. Seger's decision beat (pp. 251, 253) belongs to the character, and here the character usually doesn't react to the choice made for them (Wyn after the clearing; Reptin at Eirwen's; Alberica at the leaf). The player character has no standing of their own. They aren't charged, aren't named as counsel, and aren't asked what they think was right (first-time player note 1). The two choices that could make the player the hero of the climax (who speaks, whom to elect) are hidden or overruled. Seger's passive-protagonist warning (p. 232) bites at the climax: the verdict happens *to* the party. Berger's test (p. 94) passes for the trial choices (different outcomes) and fails for the verdict (no outcome you can see change). The bible's "fan to peer" arc for the player has no beat in this chapter. Tamsin, whom the bible has "stand by you at the Trials", doesn't appear at all.

## 7. Game fit

**Story as reward or wall?** Mostly a reward, and that's the chapter's design strength: the trials *are* the side quests, so story arrives as the payoff for doing things (Berger p. 35). The exceptions are the walls of text: the cells (34 boxes with two inputs), the escort and Fane (38 boxes; notes §E24), and the 539-character verdict list. Those are lean-back stretches inside a lean-forward week (Berger p. 45).

**Scene/sequel rhythm (Mills pp. 108–109).** Each trial has the gameplay half (goal → conflict) but rarely the **disaster**: every check's right answer comes first (notes §E6), the golden path never loses a hearing, and two fights "go on win or lose". With no disaster there's no sequel to write, so the cutscenes after each trial are celebration, not reaction → dilemma → decision. The one real dilemma, Meat Meat's "Escape?", is the best-shaped scene in the part.

**Does the story's conflict match the gameplay challenge?** Rarely. The conflict is legal and social: persuade a divided city, prove what Summer was. The gameplay is mostly wild battles in tunnels and menu checks. Where they do match, the chapter is good:
- The wall and wolves (warmth holding against the cold, by labour) fits the pillar and the city's opinion.
- Gym 8's Crystals' Dream ("Stand in the cold and don't let go of each other") is a trial by ordeal that fits Selrae's character.
- Autumn's mirror is Meat Meat breaking something to show she can free people, which fits her charge.
- The Underdark crawl, the Undercity, the Wall trip and the Drowned Hall are fights or puzzles with no bearing on any charge. They're Seger's "adding chases to fix a lull" (p. 94).

The missing match is a **courtroom mechanic for the climax**. The bible's witness count is a good idea (story as earned evidence), but the verdict spends it as a total and never asks the player to *present* anything. A small "present your evidence" beat per charge at the verdict (pick which witness answers Selrae's objection) would turn the counter into play, and Berger's "light branching that unlocks new gameplay" (p. 100) is exactly that.

**Are the choices meaningful (Berger p. 94)?**
- *Trial choices* (Anduin's tests, Meat Meat's cell, Talion, the colony): **yes**. Each has different outcomes, though shown only in branches the golden path can't reach because the right answer is always first.
- *Who speaks for the Heralds* (10.42): **no**. A hidden score with no visible change; the speaker says nothing. This is Berger's cosmetic choice (p. 99).
- *Call the verdict early* (10.41): **meaningful but a trap**. It ends open trials without saying so. Berger calls the related failure, a door that leads only to a fail state, a "false choice" (p. 36).
- *The Vote* (10.43): **meaningful in code** (it decides the Chapter 12 crossing, Rheven's griffons, Eirwen's servants' stair) and **invisible in play**. The candidates don't speak, the Act III stakes aren't named, and the player's second pick lost with no line saying why (notes §E9). Berger's rule is that a choice must lead to a different outcome, move the plot or reveal something. This one does the first two off screen and fails to tell the player it's doing either. Fix: the design already has the column ("If elected, in Act III"). Put one line of it in each candidate's mouth.

## 8. Verdict

### What's working

1. **Meat Meat's trial is a model payoff.** The deed (8.13, "she picks up the cocoon instead"), the confession that quotes it ("Meat Meat could have caught his hand. Meat Meat didn't."), a real dilemma ("Escape?"), a planted reveal (the hooded woman, 9.08 → "...Mama?"), an atonement in action (the mirror), and a verdict that turns on what Darrus meant by "It's fuel". It's set up three ways and paid three ways (Seger pp. 83, 148).
2. **The reunions carry the found-family pillar.** Anduin, Phildendrin and Talion each walk in from beyond the Wall to judge someone they love, and all three scenes land on one quiet line ("You came up spitting fish.", "My name is Phildendrin.", "He was nine.") instead of speeches, as the bible's tone rule asks.
3. **The case against Summer is built from good, specific evidence.** The children weighed ("My boy was light"), Naerth's "A." in the margins, "She ran BOTH sides", the bubble's peeling face, "The tall gold man had no reflection". Each piece comes from a different source and a different delivery (a refugee, a ledger, a room, a memory, a ghost), which is exactly Berger's Act II advice: repeat the key fact through different vehicles (p. 90).
4. **Act III is seeded with a light hand.** The pilgrim (Sephek, undisclosed), Alberica's hidden omens, and Selrae's "She'll come for what she thinks is hers" all point north without a lore dump.

### Top 3 story problems

**1. The Trials try the wrong things.** Two of five charges match something the Herald did on screen (Meat Meat, Wyn). Reptin's rests on worship we're only told about, plus a Forge deed that never happened. Alberica's is a sneeze with no setup. Alerion has no charge, and on the golden path his trial skips his own guilt (the necromancy that made Talion). Meanwhile the deeds that actually happened go untried: Glimmer killed the engine, Avarice faked her death, Selrae let the Halo in ("She'll answer for it at the Trials" is an explicit, broken promise), and Alerion promised Meat Meat "We'll talk about that later". **Why it hurts:** the chapter is the Act II climax, and a climax should cash in what the act set up. Instead it opens new, weaker threads and leaves the strongest ones lying (Seger pp. 148–150; G checklist p. 160: "Did any setups disappear?"). **Fix:**
- *Alberica*: keep the spores as the comic B-charge but plant it. Give her one beat on the walk south (9.09) tucking a lantern-cap into a cold child's hood, so "I sneezed" is a lie the player saw. Then add the real charge, which needs no new setup: Bryn Shander (Ch2), where she flooded a crowd's minds with her pendant. "SELRAE: She reached into a hundred heads without asking. On a lottery night." *(suggestion)* That's a charge only the Keeper of Memory could face, and it rhymes with Auril reaching into hers.
- *Reptin*: reword the Forge option to something the player saw ("Tell him about the acorn: you carried it north, and planted it", which is the design's own option in act2_script SC 10.02).
- *Alerion*: make the lake inquiry part of the golden path (one look before the decision; notes §E17), so he answers for "I tried to bring them all back".
- *Selrae*: put her in the dock. Gym 8 becomes her trial, not her bid (see the rewrite sketch).
- *Glimmer*: name him at the verdict (sketch beat 6).

**2. The climax has no doubt, no voice and no visible consequence.** 26 witnesses, and a Summer score of 24 against the 8 needed; one list box; three charges never read; a speaker who doesn't speak; a vote that overrules the player's pick in silence and never says what the seats will do in Act III. **Why it hurts:** an obligatory scene needs the audience to fear the outcome (Mills p. 129: raise doubt that the hero will succeed; Seger p. 48: the climax answers the central question, and here the question was never in play). A choice the player can't see the result of isn't experienced as a choice (Berger pp. 94, 99). **Fix:** at Kelyn (10.41), show the stakes per charge ("Summer: the court is nearly with you. Autumn: it's close. Bring me the camp."). Let at least one charge run close on the golden path, for instance by making the Autumn charge count only Autumn evidence, so the refugee and mirror work decides it. Stage the verdict as testimony (sketch below). At the vote, each candidate says what their seat means for the trip north, and the design's rule (4+ witnesses = both your picks win, act2_script SC 10.09) is honoured or explained.

**3. Everyone is a passenger at their own trial, the player most of all.** The player makes the accused's decisions for them (Meat Meat's stay, Wyn's defence, Reptin's answer, Alerion's goodbye), and the accused then don't react (Wyn silent after being cleared; Reptin silent at Eirwen's; Alberica silent at her own leaf). The Heralds are also invisible all week (notes §E1). The player character has no standing in the court and no stake in the outcome, and Tamsin, the bible's friend who stands by you, is absent. **Why it hurts:** Seger's decision beat is the moment that reveals a character most (p. 251), and he warns that writers skip the emotional-response beat after it. Here both are handed to the player and the response is dropped. A protagonist must act, not be acted upon, by the midpoint (Seger p. 232). **Fix:** after each player-made decision, give the accused one line of their own reaction (the notes' dialogue table has Wyn's: "...I want it. Thank you, Captain."). Give the player a role in court: Kelyn in 10.01, "And you. You travel with them. You'll speak for them, or against them." (notes, first-time player 1). Make the "who speaks" choice put that Herald on their feet for one line. Bring Tamsin into the gallery for one beat at the verdict *(suggestion: "TAMSIN: [Full Name]! You're IN THE WALL and you're on TRIAL. This is the best week of my life.")*.

### Rewrite sketch: 10.42 The Verdict (score 3), with Gym 8 refolded

The weakest important scene is the Verdict: it's the Act II obligatory scene and it plays as one list box.
