# 00 · Prologue: The Lantern (story review)

Reviewed from the as-played script `docs/review/script/00.md` (New Journey, the test's first-option path), against `docs/story/story_bible.md` (Prologue section, the one-page story, the five rules, You and Tamsin) and `docs/story/act1_script.md` SC 0.01–0.07. Line, staging and bug findings are in `docs/review/notes/00.md` and are referred to by number, not repeated. Book cites use the PDF page markers in the skill's `sources/text/`.

The prologue is also the game's opening, so it carries two jobs: the prologue's own small story (your first Pokémon day), and the inciting incident for the whole game. Most of this review is about the second job.

---

## 1. The chapter in one line

**As the player would state it from the script alone:** "Will I get my first Pokémon today?" Yes: Wynsnow hands one over and Tamsin beats you with the one that counters it (0.07–0.09). Then, in the last two scenes, a second question arrives: **"What's inside the lantern, and where did it just take me?"** The prologue gives no answer. It ends on a white screen (0.13), and Chapter 1 starts with "You land on frozen planks."

**Does the game ever *say* the question?** Never. The nearest thing is Tamsin's cut-off "Hey... [Name]? What are you..." (0.12), which is a question about *you*, and the screen goes white before it ends.

**Does it answer the question the bible gives it?** The bible's question for the prologue is "How did I get here?" As played, the prologue *shows* the cause (a cracked lantern of "real Reversal glass") but never says what the glass does or that you went into it (notes E2). The player gets the cause without knowing it's a cause. So the prologue half-answers its own question, and the answer only makes sense in hindsight, at 6.01 ("The cracked lantern on the cobbles bursts like a soap bubble").

**The game-wide central question.** The bible's throughline is "who called the Heralds north, and why". That belongs to Chapter 1, so it's fair that the prologue doesn't ask it. But the prologue *does* plant the player's own game-long question, and it's a good one:

> TAMSIN: Hellen says nobody's ever known who that is. Not even the carver. (0.11, about the faceless sixth figure)

"Who is the sixth figure?" is answered at 12.13 ("That's you. On Hellen's wall. The sixth figure.") and in the epilogue, 13.22 (your name cut under it). That is a strong, disguised setup in Seger's sense (p. 148–150): set up as a tavern curiosity, paid off as identity. Two other disguised setups are just as good: "Gold light ... It's been gold every morning of your life" (0.03) is the stopped summer of Act II, and "The ground shudders. Once." (0.12) is the forge's heat-pulse, paid off at 6.22 ("The same shudder as the lantern").

What the prologue does **not** plant is the question the inciting incident naturally raises for the player: *where am I, when am I, and how do I get home?* Nobody in Chapters 1–5 asks it (a search of scripts 01–05 for "home", "four hundred", "future" finds nothing aimed at you; Alerion's "Nobody else saw. Keep it that way." at 1.01 is the only acknowledgement). The player leaves the prologue with a fall and no goal. That is the main story problem this part hands to Chapter 1 (see §8).

**Game-wide milestones in this part.**

- **Inciting incident (Berger p. 81):** 0.12–0.13, the lantern and the fall. It's in the right place and at the right time: by Berger's measure (Act 1 "can be as short as 15 minutes", p. 90) the prologue's 64 boxes and one battle are about right. But as played it **doesn't land**: the fall isn't shown, the glass isn't explained, and the event happens *to* the player rather than *through* anything they do.
- **Introduction to the protagonist's purpose (Berger p. 81–82):** missing for the player. "Poorly written heroes do not have defined goals." Your goal in the prologue (a Pokémon) is met before the fall, and nothing replaces it after.
- **Refuse / accept the call, point of no return:** not in this part, and (see 01.md and 02.md) never given to the player at all. The game's point of no return falls in Chapter 2 (Sephek's ultimatum, 2.08).

The bible's own rule, "You are silent, and your choices are your voice", needs at least one choice here.

**Tone: "cosy, then cold".** The prologue is the cosy half and does it well: Hellen's food, Tamsin's speed, Wynsnow's dry jokes. The *cold* is supposed to arrive at 0.12–0.13, and that's where the tone fails: the town theme keeps playing through the shudder and the fall (notes E8), the white screen has no sound, and the first cold line ("The sky has no sun in it at all") is in the next chapter. The bible's tone rule says "Light banter that stops dead when the wind picks up." The banter never stops dead; it just cuts away.

## 7. Game fit

- **Story as reward or wall?** A short wall (three menus and two cards, the weakest writing in the part, come first), then mostly reward: the starter for reaching the College, the battle, the mural for coming home. From 0.10 to 0.13 the game takes control away four times in a row, so the prologue's climax is a lean-back stretch inside a lean-forward game (Berger pp. 45, 150).
- **Scene/sequel rhythm (Mills p. 108; skill §Scenes).** The battle is the one gameplay "scene" (goal: win; conflict: Tamsin's counter; disaster: you lose). Its sequel is Wynsnow's heal and Tamsin's gloat, but there's no *reaction* from you or anyone to your new partner (notes E7), and the dilemma/decision step is skipped. The bigger disaster, the fall, has no sequel in the prologue at all; its reaction has to come in 1.01, and there it's Alerion's, not yours.
- **Does the story's conflict match the gameplay challenge?** In the prologue the only conflict is the friendly rivalry, and the only challenge is the rival battle, so they match. The inciting incident has no gameplay at all; that's acceptable for a cliffhanger, but it's why it needs one player input (below).
- **Are the choices meaningful (Berger p. 94)?** The starter choice is meaningful in play (it changes your team and Tamsin's for the whole game). The mode choice is the most meaningful choice in the part and comes before the player knows anything (notes E4). The loss/win of the first battle changes one line. Nothing the player chooses touches the story.
- **Herald Mode vs New Journey.** Herald Mode's openings are better *story* openings: each starts on a mystery object and a want ("Most things you throw out don't come back"; "Tonight Razzy is gone"), so the throughline question is asked in the first minute. The two catalysts rhyme, unused: the Heralds each get a cold object they didn't ask for; you get a cracked lantern you didn't ask for.

## 8. Verdict

### What's working

1. **The setups are disguised and long-range.** The sixth figure, "gold every morning" and the shudder are planted as tavern colour and festival texture, and all three pay off in Acts II–III. That's Seger's best kind of setup (p. 148–150), and it's rare in an opening.
2. **Tamsin is a real character in four scenes.** Motivated by action, fast, funny, and the gift at the end gives the fall an emotional cost: the friend who bought you the thing that took you away.
3. **The prologue is short.** About fifteen minutes to the inciting incident, which is where Berger wants it (p. 90).
4. **Meat Meat's Herald Mode opening (SC 0.07)** is a model for the rest: want, wound, stakes and voice in nine boxes.

### Top 3 story problems

**1. The inciting incident happens off screen and to a passenger.** *Why it hurts:* the fall is the event "everything downstream is affected" by (Berger p. 81), and as played the player doesn't see it, doesn't cause it, and isn't told it happened. The white screen is the most important moment in the game and the least dramatised. *Fix:* give the player the last action. After the vision, the lantern pulses; the player presses A on it (or takes one step toward the crack) and that input starts the pull. Then show the pull: the music cuts, your sprite is drawn into the light, one line says you fall in, and the white holds into Targos (notes E2, E8 for the mechanics). A single input turns "done to you" into "you looked", which is the protagonist taking the initiative (Mills p. 59) without a false choice. *Cite:* Berger p. 81 (inciting incident), Seger p. 41–42 (an action catalyst is strongest), Seger p. 232 (passive protagonist).

**2. The prologue sets up the genre, not the story, so nothing the player sees in the glass means anything.** *Why it hurts:* Seger's "sets up everything but the story" (p. 37–43). By the fall the player knows a starter, a rival and a carving, but not who the Heralds were, what the Reversal was, or that Reversal glass holds old moments. So "Five figures coming down a gangplank" is just five people, and Chapter 1's five strangers aren't recognisable as the legends from the wall. *Fix:* three short story beats on the golden path, each inside a scene that already exists, each given to a character who'd say it: (a) Hellen names the five at the mural and gives one deed ("Five strangers who walked into the winter and came out with spring", notes first-time Q7); (b) the seller says what the glass does ("That glass saw the Heralds' winter. It remembers.", notes E2a); (c) the panel line ties the dock to the wall ("Five figures, like the five on Hellen's wall."), once the owner settles who the carved five are (§5). Exposition scattered and given to the people who care about it (Seger p. 136–142). *Cite:* Seger p. 37–43, 136–142.

**3. The player leaves the prologue with no goal, and the game never gives them one.** *Why it hurts:* the fall's natural question, "how do I get home?", is never asked in Acts I (searches of 01–05 find no line about it), so for five chapters the player follows the Heralds' goal with none of their own. That's the root of the passenger feeling in Chapters 1–2 (see 01.md §4). Berger: "Poorly written heroes do not have defined goals" (p. 81–82); Seger: a goal needs risk and difficulty (p. 227). *Fix (suggestion, new invention):* plant the way home in the prologue so it points the same way as the Heralds' lures. The glass is "from the deep roots ... where the old hag's mirrors broke". If the prologue makes that line clear, then in Chapter 1 the player's goal (find the hag's mirrors, the glass came from them) and the Heralds' goal (find who's calling them, south-east) converge on Dougan's Hole and the Cackling Chasm. The Chasm is, in fact, where the mirrors break and time comes loose (Ch5). The player would then *choose* to go with the Heralds for a reason of their own. *Cite:* Berger p. 81–82, 110 (personal and professional goals that meet); Seger p. 227.

### Rewrite sketch: 0.12–0.13, "Reversal glass" and "The fall"
