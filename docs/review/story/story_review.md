# Story review: the whole game, against the craft books

*How this was done.* The other tabs judge the game line by line: staging, pacing, dialogue, bugs. This one judges the story itself, using the eight craft books in the game-narrative skill. Nine readers took one slice each of the as-played script (the prologue to the epilogue, plus both sets of side quests). Each checked their chapters for:

- the shape of the plot (Seger's nested three acts);
- the four questions every scene has to answer, scored 1–10, where anything under 8 needs rework (Mills, pp. 108, 110, 189–191);
- the game's five fixed story moments (Berger);
- character arcs, and setups and payoffs.

The per-chapter reviews are in `docs/review/story/00.md`–`15.md`. This page pulls them together.

All findings come from what the game actually plays, not from the story bible. Where the two differ, the gap is itself a finding. Anything marked *(suggestion)* is new material that doesn't exist yet, and it's your call.

## In short

**The story is good. The player isn't in it.**

The bible's design holds up when you test it against the books:

- **The theme is stated in the story's own words, at the moment it costs the most.** Shimmer's "There's always a third way" (Ch3) becomes the ending's answer to "What do we do with a god?" (Ch13). That is Seger's rule exactly: the climax answers the question the setup raised (pp. 43, 48–49).
- **Several turns are built properly:** the Reversal (Ch5), the Forge (Ch8) and Frostina's capture (Ch11).
- **Some setups are first-class.** The faceless sixth figure on Hellen's wall (0.11) pays off as "That's you" (12.13). The gold light "every morning of your life" (0.03) turns out to be the stopped summer.

What doesn't hold up is the **player's part in it**. Every reader, independently, found the same thing in their slice: the big moments happen *to* the player, or are done by a Herald while the player watches. The examples include:

- the fall;
- the last mirror;
- Shimmer's night;
- the act turn;
- Glimmer's sacrifice;
- the dragon chase;
- the Trials;
- the final choice.

Across 441 scenes the average score is **5.8 out of 10**. Only **61 scenes (14%)** reach Mills's bar of 8.

Most low scores have one of three causes:

- **Nobody is pursuing a goal.** The player has none of their own, and the Heralds' goal goes quiet for chapters at a time.
- **Things are learned that were never set up.** The rule or object arrives in the scene that uses it.
- **The stakes are said instead of raised.** The threat is announced, and then nothing gets worse.

## What's working, game-wide

1. **The theme is carried by the plot, not by speeches.**
   - "There's always a third way" is first said by Shimmer as she dies (3.07).
   - It is tested as a choice between two wrongs at the Act I climax (5.20).
   - It becomes the final ending (13.18).
   - "Nothing that is still can grieve." / "I'd rather grieve. It means I had something." (Ch6) is the clearest statement of the foundation pillar.
2. **The act breaks are stitched on both sides.**
   - Thredra's "Mirrors always remember." becomes the cracked-mirror seal on the Summer decree in the very next scene (6.04).
   - The twins' "WAIT" in the fall becomes Razzilith's "...WAITED."
   - Kelyn's "Half of them think you saved them. Half think you killed it." (9.10) *is* Chapter 10's question, said by the person who will judge it.
3. **The best character work is shown in actions.**
   - Reptin's curse goes from bite to charge in five beats, ending on "It knows me now" (Ch4–5).
   - Meat Meat lets Darrus fall and picks up the cocoon (8.13). Her trial then quotes it back: "Meat Meat could have caught his hand. Meat Meat didn't." (Ch10). Ch10's reader calls it "set up three ways and paid three ways".
   - Sephek weeps after killing Shimmer: "I only held the knife. I'm sorry." That makes his turn in Act III possible.
4. **The mirror pillar works as a rule, not a decoration.** Examples:
   - the boy's smirking reflection and Meat Meat's burned hands (Ch7);
   - the coat motif ("Var was a coat!");
   - the three Albericas in the endings.
5. **The game responds to the player in the places that count.** The fed wolf pack, Talion, Eline, the shard choice, the Vote and the Wheel all come back in Act III (Ch11, Ch13). This is Berger's reactive narrative (pp. 111–112).
6. **The side quests hold the best warmth and the most choices.** The Ten-Towns quests have about thirty real decisions (14). The Cycle Canticle (15) is a model subplot: set up in Act II, chosen in the theme's own words, paid off in the final battle.

## The five story problems

### 1. The player is a passenger in their own story

**What happens.** The player's inciting incident, the fall into the lantern, happens behind a white screen. Nothing they did causes it, and no line says it happened (0.13). After that:

- one line in Targos acknowledges it ("You fell out of the air. ...Nobody else saw. Keep it that way.", 1.01);
- nobody asks where or when they are, or how they get home, in Chapters 1–5;
- the player never chooses to go with the Heralds;
- the Act I climax is solved by Alberica after a staff breaks itself, and the last mirror breaks in narration (5.23–5.24);
- Glimmer's sacrifice, the dragon chase, the Trials and the final choice are all watched (8.21, 9.04–9.07, 10.42, 13.18).

The bible's own rule for the player, "Auril can't read you, because you aren't on her wheel", is never used.

**Why it hurts.** Seger: by the midpoint at the latest the protagonist must act on the story, not be pushed by it (p. 232); the moment of decision reveals the most character, and it's the beat scripts most often leave out (p. 251). Berger: "Poorly written heroes do not have defined goals" (pp. 81–82), and the catharsis belongs to the protagonist, not to a side character (p. 111). Mills: at the climax, strip away what the hero relied on, so they win with what the journey taught them (p. 132).

**The fix** has three parts:

1. **A goal of the player's own, from the prologue.** *(Suggestion.)* The lantern's glass came "from the deep roots ... where the old hag's mirrors broke". Make that clear in the prologue. Then the player's way home (find the hag's mirrors) and the Heralds' lures (south-east, to the hag) point at the same place, Dougan's Hole. The two goals meet at the Act I climax, and the player chooses to stay at the act turn (problem 2).
2. **The player's hand on every turn.** These are small inputs that don't change the outcome, plus one real choice per act. The chapter sketches give them:
   - press A on the lantern to start the fall (00);
   - break the last mirror yourself, and choose the acorn (05);
   - choose who keeps the watch the night Shimmer dies (03);
   - at the Forge, a real move at "Your move." and a hand for Glimmer (08);
   - choose how to fight the dragon over Bryn Shander: draw it off over the lake, or hold the square under its wings. These are two rights with different costs (09, Mills p. 129).
3. **Use the bible's player rule at the climax.** *(Suggestion, from the Ch13 sketch.)* Auril's last cold stills everyone on her wheel: the Heralds, the dragons, Sephek. Only the player can move. The player chooses the ending by carrying the diamond to someone. The hero's supports are stripped away, and the third way is done with only what the journey taught. This also pays off "you aren't on her wheel" and the sixth figure: the one nobody could name is the one outside time.

### 2. The game's five big story moments are in the right places, but two of them don't land

Berger's five fixed stakes (pp. 81–87, 134–136), as the game plays them:

| Milestone | Where | Lands? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inciting incident | 0.12–0.13, the lantern and the fall | **No.** It's in the right place and is about the right length (Act 1 "can be as short as 15 minutes", p. 90). But it plays off screen, with no line, no sound and no movement. The Heralds get a second inciting incident in 1.02–1.04 ("That's not luck. That's bait."), and that one lands. |
| Point of no return | For the Heralds, 2.07–2.08: the lottery stand and Sephek's ultimatum, "before the next moon, or I choose someone from this square instead". For the player, nowhere. | **Half.** The Heralds' commitment lands. Its consequence, Auril's promised storm, never arrives, and the scene where the Dale reacts (the Council) is hidden and expires (02). The game's real act turn, the Reversal (5.24), happens *to* the party. The Wardens' oath (6.04) commits nobody to anything, and Act II has no mission until the last scene of Chapter 6 (06). |
| Midpoint / low point | 8.13–8.21, the Forge. Meat Meat lets Darrus fall, Glimmer dies, and the heroes' own victory launches the dragon. | **Yes**, in the right place and caused by the heroes' own choices, as Berger wants (pp. 83–84). It is weakened because its reveal ("Summer is Avarice") was spent in Chapters 6–7 (problem 4). |
| Ticking clock | 11.06, Frostina taken: "She's still beating. Far. North." | **Set, then switched off.** Nothing says *when*, nothing gets worse if you wait, and the White Vault detour spends the urgency in Chapter 12 (11, 12). |
| Climax | 13.16–13.18, Auril and "What do we do with a god?" | **Yes as theme, no as act.** It answers the question in the story's own words. The player picks from a menu, though, and every earned bonus is cashed in rather than taken away (13). |

**The fixes:**

- **Point of no return.** Give the player one *(suggestion)*: at the empty throne in 6.04, Selrae says the Echo Gate opens on the Dale ("Out there it's been weeks. You could be home by supper."), and Reptin says Anduin's name. The choice is "We'll guard the pulse." or "We're going home." Going home walks you to the Gate and the frozen Dale, and you turn back. Either way the party commits, but now the player chose it (06 sketch). Mills's first doorway is "a threshold she chooses to cross with no way back" (pp. 29, 112).
- **Ticking clock.** Give it a date the world already owns. *(Suggestion.)* "She'll do it on the longest night." Let one or two people count the nights down, and move the White Vault back to the postgame, where the bible puts it (11, 12). Mills: give a deadline, then cut it in half (p. 192).
- **Act I's missing consequence.** Fade the lottery night out on "Morning. The square is empty. The board is still up." and put the Council on the path as the morning after (02). Berger: every major beat needs consequences later (pp. 112–113).

### 3. Payoffs arrive without their setups, and setups wait on optional paths

Seger's test runs both ways: every payoff set up, every setup paid off (pp. 148–150). The game is strong on disguised setups, but too many key objects and rules arrive in the scene that uses them, or live where the main path doesn't go.

**Payoffs with no setup on the path:**

- **The unicorn horn.** It first appears when Shade gives it up for Razzy (Ch2).
- **The naming rule** ("a name, spoken as a promise"). It is announced when it's needed (2.04).
- **The carved cat and Shimmer's song.** Both are recalled at her death (3.08), but they were only shown in the optional rest scene, 1.07.
- **The ghost's rule, "a life not yet begun".** The Act I climax depends on it, and it lives in an optional room (5.18).
- **Alberica's charge at the Trials.** It is a sneeze nobody saw.
- **Reptin's Forge deed.** The trial cites it, but it never happened (10).

**Setups that never pay:**

- **The way home.** It is never asked.
- **Auril's storm after the lottery.** It is never sent.
- **A dozen festival-week mysteries** (06): the note, the charm, the petals, the blue lights.
- **`knows_xardorok` and `corsair_arms_deal`.** These flags are read by nothing (07).
- **Most of the personal quests in Act II.** The bible moved them into personal quests "so they feed the Trials", but the Verdict reads four of them, all for one charge (15).

**The best setups, worth protecting:**

- **The sixth figure** (0.11 → 12.13, 13.22).
- **"Gold every morning"** (0.03 → Act II's stopped summer).
- **The shudder** (0.12 → 6.22, "The same shudder as the lantern").
- **"Mirrors always remember"** (5.27 → 6.04).
- **The second heartbeat** (9.03 → 11.06 → 13.04).

**Motifs with three or more appearances on the main path:**

- Shimmer's four notes;
- mirrors and lag;
- the coat;
- hold and keep;
- the gold dawn;
- the laughter.

Some of these change as they recur, which Seger likes (pp. 155–156). In the Ch5 sketch, the laughter turns from the hag's to the yeti cub's.

**Fix.** Do one setup pass per act, before the scene rewrites in fix-plan steps 4–6. For each payoff in the table above, plant its setup earlier, on the path, inside a scene that already exists, in a character's own voice. The chapter reviews name a place for each:

- **Shade's horn:** a table line in Ch1 ("I pulled her out of the snow in Saltmarsh. There was a horn lying there. I kept it.").
- **The naming rule:** Siv's "The cold keeps our real ones" on the path in 2.03.
- **The cat and the song:** 1.07 on the spine, by sending you to rest before the gate.
- **The ghost's rule:** the sanctum as the way to the Heart.
- **Alberica's charge:** the real one, her pendant reaching "into a hundred heads without asking", which needs no new setup (Ch2).

Then check the reverse: for every flag the game sets, either something reads it or the setup gets cut.

### 4. Act II gives its own twist away, and its middle sags

The bible's Chapter 8 question is "Who is Summer?" The as-played game answers it at least six times before Chapter 8 can ask it, in both the main path and the side content:

- Razzilith's "Summer is a woman. A tiefling." (6.17);
- Selrae's "Avarice." (7B.12);
- Var's vision of Avarice taking him;
- the lab notes, the Scriptory and the pages;
- "a horned woman with an A" in at least six optional scenes (15).

So "The face cracks like a mirror" (8.06) is a confirmation, not a reversal. Three lines also contradict the reveal:

- Grik describes a face nobody has seen;
- Kelyn's "Var? thank the seasons";
- Razzilith's line (08).

Around the reveal, Act II sags:

- **Six identical festival nights**, advanced by resting, on a clock with no threat on it (06).
- **Road B's lower zones:** eighteen scenes across six maps, averaging 4.8 (07).
- **Two parallel roads that never fold back into a scene.** The door opens in six silent boxes, and the order you take them in changes two lines (07).
- **Ten seers and both captains** all deliver the same warning (15).

**Why it hurts.** A twist has to be a path the audience was invited down, not one they were told to take (Seger, pp. 86–88). A villain overexposed before the boss falls flat (Berger, p. 88). Momentum is cause and effect, not more incidents (Seger, pp. 79–80, 94). And repetition past three becomes noise (Seger, p. 83).

**The fix.** First, choose twist or irony (your call):

- **Twist.** Keep one ambiguous clue per road (Hyphora's "A woman who counts"; "Summer is a coat"). Move Selrae's naming to 8.06. Move Var's vision to after the Ch8 fight, where "She's wearing me, isn't she" becomes grief, not exposition. Turn the lab notes into Trials evidence.
- **Irony.** Let the player know, and let the city not know. Then Kelyn's welcome for Var and the Council's trust become dread instead of contradiction.

Either way, fix the three contradicting lines. Then shorten the middle:

- three festival nights instead of six, each with one player action;
- Road B's lower zones as one three-room sequence (Seger's scene sequence, pp. 90–93);
- a meeting scene at the door that reads both roads' flags. The 07 sketch writes it: Wyn finally asks Hyphora "You called me away. That night. Why?", and gets "Ask me when the burning stops."

### 5. Each Herald's arc is written, but the key beats sit where the path doesn't go

The bible gives every Herald a want, a strength that is also a flaw, and an arc. The arcs are good on paper. As played, the beats a player can actually see are uneven:

| Character | What the player sees | What's missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **You** | A fan in the prologue. "That's you" on the wall (12.13). Tamsin's goodbye (12). | A goal, and the decisions (problem 1). Tamsin vanishes between the prologue and Ch12; the bible has them "stand by you at the Trials", but they aren't there (10). |
| **Shimmer** | 21 boxes before her death, across three chapters. Never on screen while you walk, never in a fight, never in a scene with anyone but Shade. | Time with her before the loss. Berger: slow the pace to build the bond before a tragedy (pp. 103–104, 118). The 03 sketch moves the cat and the hum onto the path, and gives her one exchange with Alberica, who "heard her last". |
| **Shade / Glimmer** | Excellent foreshadowing: the horn, the cat, Luna, "Glimmer. That name again." A death that's earned (6.08 → 8.14 → 8.21 → 9.03). | The transformation is off stage and not Shade's choice. Shade leaves a newly hatched sister for seven years, and the player's "Go." / "Come back." does nothing (06). Glimmer has no scene of his own before he dies (07). |
| **Reptin** | The best Act I arc (Ch4–5). The reunion with Anduin (Ch10). | The near-god fear in Act II is all warmth, with no cost (15). His trial cites a deed that never happened, and his Test rewards his flaw without touching Anduin or the tree (10, 13). |
| **Alberica** | Marked at the lottery (Ch2). The Still Keeper and the three Albericas (Ch13). | The rune goes quiet at the Act I climax (05). At the Trials she's charged with a sneeze. Her temptation in the Test is a stranger, when it could be her mother's laugh or Shimmer (13). |
| **Alerion** | The Test of Isolation, which pays off three setups in his own voice. It's the model for the others (13). | No charge at the Trials. On the main path his trial skips his own guilt, the necromancy that made Talion (10). |
| **Meat Meat** | The model arc: the pens, Autumn falls, the trial, "Meat Meat owes you NOTHING", the Test of Cruelty. | Ending A makes her the weapon her Test refused, then gives her the same happy epilogue (13). |
| **Wyn** | Road A, where the puzzles are acts of care (07). | Their question to Hyphora is never asked, and they say nothing after they're cleared (07, 10). |
| **Thredra** | "I sent them." (4.22). | Her motive splits between Ch4 ("I'm only her seamstress") and Ch5 ("Not her winter. Mine."), and nobody connects the two. | **Avarice** | A good engineer-villain at the Forge. | Overexposed in Acts II and III (problem 4, and the Vault in Ch12). She stands idle through the Forge's climax. | **Sephek** | A sympathetic antagonist and a hero of his own story. He weeps, he can be turned, and "I'm sorry. I'm taking her home." | Little. He is the cast's best-built antagonist. |

**The fix.** Seger's arc rule is that even a small change needs five to seven beats the audience can see (pp. 255–258). For each Herald, list the arc's beats from the bible. Then check each one is on the path, on screen and played as an action, and move or add the ones that aren't. The 10 and 13 reviews give the Trial and Test rewrites one by one. The rule for both is that each must try or test something the player watched that Herald do.

The highest-scoring parts are the ones where gameplay carries the story:

- **Ten-Towns side quests (6.5).** Choices are this part's whole currency.
- **The Chasm (6.4).** The troll heals unless burned, the locket counters the hag's illusion, and the yeti you calmed fights beside you.

That is the clearest single lesson in the review. In this game the story works when the player is doing something that *is* the story.

## Theme and tone

- **Small warmth against a vast cold.** It's strongest in the side quests: the offering motif, the cookies, Good Mead's honey cakes, Hellen's food in the Test of Cruelty. It's weakest where a bounty or a joke lands on top of grief. For example, the White Moose ask comes straight after "Shimmer's bedroll is empty" (14), and Shade is quipping one chapter after their sister dies (04).
- **Found family across time.** It works in Act II's reunions: Anduin, Phildendrin and Talion each land on one quiet line ("You came up spitting fish.", "My name is Phildendrin.", "He was nine."). It's missing in Act I, where the five are introduced by their relics. It's also missing in the long gap where Tamsin should be.
- **Every mirror lies a little.** This is the most consistent pillar, and it gets stronger as the game goes. The 04 sketch turns the three identical mirror fights into three truths, one per Herald, so the rule shows itself instead of repeating.
- **Change over stillness.** This is the foundation, stated clearly three times: by Shimmer (Ch3), in the Rootspire exchange (Ch6) and at the final choice (Ch13). The endings test it unevenly. The Shatter ending costs too little, and the Loop costs a full Act III replay without warning, so the Third Way is found by elimination rather than chosen (13). Berger calls this a false choice (p. 36).
- **"Cosy, then cold."** The tone rhythm is right scene by scene in Chapters 3, 5 and 9. Elsewhere, long runs of banter without a cold turn flatten it, because a scene with no emotional shift is flat (Seger, p. 144).

## Game fit

- **Story as a wall versus a reward.** Berger's rule is never to push a lean-forward player into lean-back (pp. 45, 150). Runs of up to 58 boxes go by on the main path without a choice, a walk or a battle. The fix plan's box limits (no run over 25) are the craft rule here too.
- **Scene and sequel.** Mills maps the scene (goal, conflict, disaster) onto gameplay and the sequel (reaction, dilemma, decision) onto the cutscene that follows. Three of the game's biggest scenes have no sequel:
  - the lottery night cuts to daylight (02);
  - the Forge cuts away from Glimmer's death with no grief beat (08);
  - the arrest cuts straight into the Tribunal (09).

  Each needs one quiet beat that matches the scene's intensity (Mills, pp. 108–109).
- **Meaningful choice.** The side quests are full of real choices. The main path has few, and some are cosmetic. "Go with her?" happens four times in the festival week, and Shade's "Go." / "Come back." changes nothing. Berger: every option must change the outcome, move the plot, or reveal something not obvious (p. 94). The fix is fewer, heavier choices on the spine, each read later.
- **Story conflict matches gameplay challenge** where it's built that way: the troll that only fire kills, the Endurance test's blizzard that only physical moves get through, and the chase's air battles, where each win knocks a seam off the dragon so it starts the last fight weaker. Make that the standard for every boss.

## Decisions for you

These are story calls, not craft fixes. The reviews give a view on each, but they're yours to make:

1. **Give the player a goal of their own (a way home) that meets the Heralds' goal at Dougan's Hole?** This is the central fix for problem 1. It's new material, but it uses the bible's own lantern-glass and mirror rules.
2. **Summer is Avarice: twist or irony?** (Problem 4.)
3. **Move the White Vault back to the postgame?** The code already has the branch where Avarice comes to the throne without it ("I had another in a glacier. You never came to look.").
4. **The Ten-Towns quests: keep or cut?** If you keep them, each gets one crossing into the main story. The 14 review lists them, for example the Mountain Climb's Keegan among the Ch9 refugees. A finished quest could also add a witness at the Trials.
5. **The Trials:** put Selrae in the dock (Gym 8 as her trial), and give Alberica her real charge, the pendant at the lottery?

## How this changes the fix plan

The fix plan's steps 1–3 (everyone on screen, what breaks play, goals you can follow) come first, because they change how every scene feels. Step 2 is done.

Steps 4–6 (Acts I, II and III, scene by scene) should take this review as their standard as well as the line notes. Each scene rewrite starts from its chapter's sketch. Each must pass Mills's four questions at 8 or more, and give the player at least one input on the beat that matters. Do the setup pass for the act (problem 3) before its scenes. The *(suggestion)* material waits for your answers to the decisions above.
