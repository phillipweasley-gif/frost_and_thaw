# 02 · Chapter 2: Lottery Night (story review)

Reviewed from the as-played script `docs/review/script/02.md` (New Journey, all five travelling), against the bible's Chapter 2 section, the one-page story, the five rules and the character sheets, and `act1_script.md` SC 2.01–2.09. Line, staging and bug findings are in `docs/review/notes/02.md` and are referred to by number, not repeated.

---

## 1. The chapter in one line

**As the player would state it:** "Can we save the little goblin who ran into the light, and will this town give up the girl whose name was drawn?" The answers: the goblin is kept alive inside an egg that needs a name nobody knows (2.04); the town keeps the girl because Alberica makes it remember summer (2.07). The price: Auril marks Alberica, and Sephek tells Shimmer to come to Lonelywood "before the next moon, or I choose someone from this square instead" (2.08).

**The bible's question: "What does Auril want with us?"** As played, the answer is visible but not said: she wants Shimmer back ("To the girl with the stone." / "Tell her I said no. Again.") and she has claimed Alberica (the snowflake-and-crown rune). *Why* she wants them, the vessel idea, is held for Chapter 3. That's a fair split.

**Does the game say the question?** Never in those words. What it says aloud is the egg's question: "REPTIN: The old rhymes want a name, spoken as a promise." / "ALBERICA: And none of us knows his." / "ALERION: Then we find someone who does." (2.04). That one is answered in Ch3 (3.11, Meat Meat names Razzy).

**Does it answer the question Chapter 1 raised?** No, and it doesn't pretend to. Ch1 asked "who's calling us?" and pointed at Dougan's Hole. Ch2 detours to Lonelywood because of Sephek's ultimatum. The detour is earned (a deadline with hostages is a stronger pull than a compass), but nobody in Ch2 mentions the lures or Dougan's Hole, so the Ch1 question goes quiet for a chapter. It comes back in Ch3's last scene (3.19, "The laughing is the calling").

**Game-wide milestones in this part.**

- **Point of no return (Berger p. 81): 2.07–2.08. Lands.** The first heroic act (Alberica's stand, with you beside her) commits the party: a town has broken its bargain with a goddess because of them, the goddess has put her mark on one of them, and her killer has named a deadline with hostages. After this they can't stay strangers passing through. It is also Seger's first turning point for Act I as a whole: a new direction (Lonelywood, not Dougan's Hole), the central question raised again (what does she want with us?), a decision, higher stakes and a new arena (p. 45–47). This is the strongest milestone in Parts 00–02.
- **Ticking clock: "Come before the next moon, or I choose someone from this square instead."** A genuine clock with a human cost, and it's repeated in Ch3 (3.06, Shade reading the note: "He said before the next moon, or he picks someone else."). It lands in the story. Gameplay ignores it (the player can spend hours on Kelvin's Cairn), which is normal for the genre but worth one acknowledgement in Ch3 if the player dawdles.
- **The consequence of the point of no return never arrives.** Four characters warn that breaking the bargain brings Auril's storm ("Targos skipped it once. The storm that came after...", Ch1; "Duvessa tried to stop it once. The storm that came after took three roofs."; Danneth: "and the storm comes for both of us anyway"; Duvessa: "until the next storm comes"). A search of scripts 03–05 and 09 finds no storm. See §8, problem 2.

## 7. Game fit

- **Story as reward.** The chasm is the model: a short dungeon of Aurilites, then the egg rite at the top as the reward. Berger's "story as reward for effort" (p. 35). The lottery is the opposite: it can only start through a forced rest at the Northlook, and it runs 37 boxes with one choice (notes E17). The gym then comes after the story's climax, so the chapter's last gameplay challenge has nothing at stake.
- **Scene/sequel (Mills p. 108).** The road is a clean pair: the chasm is the scene (goal: follow Razzy; conflict: the Aurilites; disaster: he falls into the light), and the rite is the sequel (reaction "He's alive"; dilemma "It wants blood"; decision "Do it" or the horn). The lottery is a full scene with **no sequel** on the golden path: no reaction beyond two lines, no dilemma, and the decision to go to Lonelywood is made by the goal text. The sequel exists, and it's good: the Council session (Duvessa owns it; the towns argue; "Bryn Shander draws no more names on my word"). It's optional and expires.
- **Story conflict vs gameplay challenge.** The chasm matches (cultists guard Auril's light). The gym doesn't: Duvessa is the chapter's ally, and her "Show me it was worth it" is the only reason to fight her. The climax has no battle, which is right: the challenge is a choice.
- **Meaningful choice (Berger p. 94).** One good one (Stand / Watch), one that tells you you're wrong ("Wait..."), one false one (the rest). The good one is in the right place, at the point of no return.

## 8. Verdict

### What's working

1. **Lottery night (2.07) is the best scene in Parts 00–02.** A dark moment, a hero's act that is also her flaw, the player's first real choice, the antagonist's mark, and a ticking clock, in one staged scene. It's the game's point of no return and it lands.
2. **The road is a clean scene/sequel pair.** Razzy's catalyst, the chasm, the rite: cause and effect, with two Heralds paying something real (blood, the horn).
3. **Character through action.** Reptin's blood, Shade's horn, Alberica's climb, Duvessa calling off the walk, Sephek's "I'm sorry": most wants in this chapter are shown in the present, which is what Ch1 lacked.
4. **Two disguised setups**: the names motif (paid by Meat Meat's "Razzy") and "some other way" (paid by Shimmer's third way).

### Top 3 story problems

**1. Two stories that don't touch.** *Why it hurts:* the egg plot (road) and the lottery plot (town) never meet in this chapter. In Bryn Shander the egg does nothing ("Keep the egg warm" is a goal with no gameplay and no scene), and the only bridge is Mishann's "You gave blood to something the cold had taken", spoken by someone who wasn't there. Seger: a subplot must push and intersect the plot (p. 63–64); momentum is each scene carrying the seed of the next (p. 79–80). *Fix (suggestion):* one physical link during 2.07. When Alberica sings the square its summer, the egg in Reptin's coat warms and glows green through the cloth (it's the memory of warmth, and it's the names motif: the cold takes, the song gives back). Mishann, staged at the crowd's edge with her lantern (notes E6), sees the glow. That's why she looks for Reptin afterwards, and it replaces "your wolf-friend" with a reason. *Cite:* Seger p. 63–64, 79–80.

**2. The point of no return has no sequel and no consequence.** *Why it hurts:* the chapter's biggest act (a town defies a goddess) ends in a cut to daylight, Ilda is never seen again, the threatened storm never comes, and the scene where the Dale reacts (the Council) is hidden and expires. Berger: every major beat needs consequences later (p. 112–113); Mills: the sequel's emotion should match the scene (p. 108–109). Without it, the defiance costs nothing but Alberica's rune. *Fix:* (a) fade the night out and open "Morning. The square is empty. The board is still up." This version moves it to the hall steps the morning after, joins the two stories, and gives the night its quiet beat. It assumes problem 1's fix (Mishann saw the egg glow in 2.07).
